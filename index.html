SportsPort

Live tournament management for amateur badminton.

Available in the App Store, on Google Play and as a web app. Built with Flutter and Firebase, in English, Thai and Chinese.

App Store Google Play Try the web app Watch the demos Full case study

The app

Amateur badminton tournaments here run on Google Sheets. Players refresh a spreadsheet between matches to find out when and where they play next. SportsPort keeps the sign-up flow organizers already use and adds what spreadsheets can't do: live standings on every phone, real-time match tracking, and a schedule that adjusts as the day runs.

  • Imports the Google Forms registration sheet directly
  • Group + knockout, Swiss + knockout, or straight knockout
  • Results update on every device instantly
  • Remaining-time estimate based on actual match pace
  • Players join by scanning a QR code, no install or account needed

What it shows about my experience

  • A full product: web dashboard and mobile app from one codebase
  • Real-time data, user accounts, roles and permissions
  • Tested by importing the complete data of two past tournaments that were run on spreadsheets
  • Brand, landing page and app-store listing done myself
  • From first prototype to submitted to both app stores in about three months

Three decisions that shaped it

  1. Two screens, one codebase

    Organizers work from a laptop, players from a phone. Instead of one layout stretched to fit both, each gets an interface designed for how they actually use it, sharing the same logic underneath.

  2. Keep the "one page" feeling

    Organizers coming from spreadsheets look for the single sheet that shows everything. A map view and a printable PDF give them exactly that, alongside the interactive app.

  3. Nothing in the way of joining

    At a casual Saturday tournament, nobody installs an app. So you don't have to: scan a QR code, see your matches. Accounts only when you want to do more.

How AI helped here

Claude knows more about software engineering than I do, but AI tends to go along with whatever you ask. If I ask for something the quick way, it will often just build it the quick way. So I gave it a standing rule for this project: whenever what I ask goes against professional best practice, it has to tell me, explain the better approach and why, and let me choose. That way it pushes back when it matters, and the decisions stay mine.

What's next: from 2027 I'll be running my own badminton tournaments on SportsPort.

ConvoGo

A face-to-face translator for two people and one phone.

Available in the App Store and on Google Play. Built with Flutter, with a small Cloudflare Workers backend.

App Store link Google Play link

The app

Two people standing face to face who don't share a language. One holds the phone, both speak, and the app shows what was said in both languages as a simple chat. No record buttons and no robot voice reading things out. You switch speaker and keep talking.

It started as a fun experiment and turned into a complete app.

  • No buttons per turn: switching speaker is the only action
  • Built to use with one thumb
  • You read instead of listen, so it works in noisy places

What it shows about my experience

  • Published on both the App Store and Google Play
  • Speech recognition and translation working together live
  • A small backend that keeps API keys off the phone
  • Designing by removing things instead of adding them

How I build with AI

I don't reinvent the way I work when I use AI. I look at how the work is already done, find the steps that follow a recipe, and let AI do the legwork on those steps. Then I check what comes out.

Start from how you already work

Most work has parts where you already know the recipe: the same steps, in the same order, every time. Those are the parts AI can take over. People often struggle to see where AI fits, but the answer is usually already in their own process. Write down the steps, have AI carry them out, and check the output until you trust it.

Let each side do what it's good at

AI is strongest where it has seen a lot of examples. Writing code, using Git, following good engineering practice: there's a huge amount of that online, and AI is often better at it than most people. But an app is more than code. Which direction should it go? What do users really want, and what would they pay for? How should it look and feel on screen? Those are judgment calls about people: what's easy to use and what's actually worth building. That's where I'm stronger than AI, and where I spend most of my time.

Manage it like an engineer on your team

I work with AI the way a manager works with a software engineer. I set the direction, ask relevant questions, and make sure it goes through the right steps. For anything big or risky that means: write a plan, have several independent reviewers critique the plan before any code exists, check whether their criticism actually holds up, revise the plan, build it, run the tests, review the code, update the documentation, and finally try it myself on a real phone. Even in areas where I'm not an expert myself, that process makes the AI find and fix its own mistakes.
